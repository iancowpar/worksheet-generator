# The Tuesday Test

**Subtitle:** *What the Starbucks AI retirement just told us about enterprise adoption*

**Status:** Final, paste-ready for Substack.

---

Starbucks fired its AI inventory system this week. Nine months in, 11,000 stores. The official line called it "a decision to standardize how inventory is counted," which is a careful way to phrase fixing something that didn't work. The internal employee note Starbucks themselves shared was more honest. "The thought behind it was great, but the execution was proving difficult."

That sentence is the whole pattern.

You can do the thinking well, and the deployment well, and the rollout, and the training, and the dashboard in front of a leadership team, and still miss the question that actually matters, which is whether the people who have to live with the system are different on Tuesday.

In the Starbucks case the workers told them in February. Reuters reported it. The complaints were specific. The tool was confusing similar milk types, skipping items, and forcing people to recount every scan to verify what the AI had said. So instead of saving time, the AI was making the floor do its job and the AI's job. Starbucks said the tool was working. Three months later they retired it.

This is the most predictable pattern in enterprise AI right now, and almost nobody is willing to name it. The deployment goes live and gets called adoption. The training session happens and gets called readiness. The vendor demo accuracy rate gets repeated in slide decks until people forget it came from a demo. Meanwhile the floor knows, the PM running their own workflow knows, the engineer who tried the tool three times and gave up knows. The signal is there. The question is whether anyone in the chain above them is set up to hear it.

The Starbucks employee note is what hearing it sounds like. "The thought behind it was great, but the execution was proving difficult." That isn't a vendor problem. It isn't even a tooling problem. It's an organization-level problem about which signals you're set up to receive.

The version of AI adoption that works in practice does the opposite of what the Starbucks rollout did. It starts closer to the floor than to the press release. It instruments behavior change instead of activity. It measures what stopped, not what launched. It pays attention to the workflows that quietly retired themselves, because that's the only honest signal that adoption is real.

The underlying frame I keep coming back to is Zero-Translation Building. The idea is straightforward. If the people who have to use the system can't operate it inside their own work without translation, the system hasn't been adopted. It's been deployed. Those are different things.

The Starbucks tool was deployed across 11,000 stores. It was never adopted at a single one.

There's an easy way to tell the difference, and most organizations could run the diagnostic in a week. Pick five workflows your AI tool is supposed to improve. Ask the people who run those workflows on a Tuesday whether they're spending less time on them than they did before. If the answer is no, or worse, more, then what you have is a deployed system, not an adopted one. Whatever the dashboard says, the floor is doing both jobs.

The harder question is what to do when the diagnostic comes back ugly. Most organizations don't have a good answer because the people closest to the truth are at the bottom of the org chart, and the people running the program are sitting on a budget line they had to defend to get funded. Admitting the deployment isn't adoption is admitting the budget line failed. So the gap widens. The press release stays, the training keeps running, the dashboard keeps reporting attendance.

This is the part that doesn't get said out loud often enough. The Starbucks story isn't really about a bad tool. It's about an organization that found a way to admit, in writing, that the floor was right. That part is rare, and most companies don't reach it. They keep the tool running because turning it off costs more politically than letting it underperform.

When the official statement called the retirement "a decision to standardize how inventory is counted," that wasn't spin. It was, in its own careful way, the most honest sentence in a corporate AI announcement this year. They retired a system because the people using it kept telling them it didn't work.

The line I'd put on the kickoff-meeting poster isn't the employee quote, though that one belongs there too. It's a question. Before any AI deployment, ask the team what would have to be true on a Tuesday, three months from now, for this to have worked. Then go check.

## Post (≈700 words)

This week Blink, an employee-engagement platform built for frontline workers,
raised $17M from Enlightened Hospitality Investments and announced a partnership
with Shake Shack.

Easy to file under "another HR-tech raise." I think it's an early read on where
durable value is going to live — and the most interesting part isn't in the
press release.

Start with who wrote the check. Enlightened Hospitality Investments sits in
Danny Meyer's orbit — the person whose entire career is one argument:
hospitality *is* the product. Take care of the people doing the work and they
take care of the people paying for it. When that specific money goes into a tool
for frontline workers, with a burger company as the proof case, it isn't a bet
on software. It's a bet that the frontline worker is the competitive edge, not
the cost line.

For two decades, software didn't believe that. We digitized knowledge work
first — the easy ROI: people at desks, on computers, with corporate email. The
~80% of the workforce who don't sit at a desk got the leftovers. Not because
they mattered less, but because they were harder to reach and easier to treat as
a number on a labor-cost spreadsheet. I spent years building software in that
world. The under-investment was never a mystery — it was a choice the market
kept making because the math kept working.

Here's what changed the math. It's not the labor shortage. That's the surface
story everyone tells. It's AI — just not in the direction people point.

The frontline shift didn't start with AI; this was being built before the
current wave. But AI did something to the *value* of work we're all still
half-pretending isn't happening: it's compressing the moat around exactly the
work we spent twenty years prioritizing. The deck, the first draft, the routine
analysis — defensible because they used to require a trained human — are getting
cheaper by the quarter. Value doesn't vanish when that happens. It moves. It
moves to the work that is stubbornly, expensively human: the thing that happens
in person, in a moment, between two people, that you cannot prompt your way to.
The shake made right. The patient who felt cared for. The store that felt like
someone was home.

That work has a name. It's the frontline.

But "AI can't do it" is only half of why this matters. The other half is what
this software is actually *for* — and it isn't communication tooling. It's
belonging. For most of the 80%, the organization has always been something that
happens elsewhere: decided in a building they don't work in, relayed through a
manager who may or may not pass it on. The thesis I've spent the last couple of
years working on is narrower and more pointed than "engagement" — democratize
belonging. Give every employee a real way to be part of the organization
regardless of where, when, or how they work. Not a town hall they can't attend.
An actual seat.

I noticed a Blink customer describe their product, almost word for word, as
"the democratization of communication." That isn't a coincidence of phrasing.
It's the same thesis surfacing independently in the market — which is usually
the sign that a thesis is about to stop being a thesis and start being a
category.

So the question isn't "is white-collar work under attack by AI." It's narrower
and more uncomfortable: AI is repricing work in two directions at once. Down,
for what it can do. Up — for the first time in a generation — for the work it
can't. And the most valuable version of the work it can't do isn't just
physical or in-person. It's relational. It's whether the person doing it feels
like they belong to something.

The companies that win the next decade won't be the ones who used AI to make
knowledge work cheaper. Everyone will do that; it won't be a moat. They'll be
the ones who took the savings and gave the 80% they spent twenty years
optimizing the one thing they were never given: a way to belong to the
organization, not just work for it. That's the part AI can't do for them. It's
also, not coincidentally, the entire premise of enlightened hospitality.

We spent two decades treating the frontline as a cost to be managed. AI may be
the thing that finally forces us to treat belonging as the edge it always was.

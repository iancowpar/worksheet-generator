I learned a word tonight. I was half-listening to a podcast. Alison Wood Brooks, a Harvard researcher who studies conversation, was on Diary of a CEO, and she said "boomerasking." I stopped what I was doing.

Boomerasking is when you ask a question that was never really a question. You ask "how was your weekend," and before the answer lands you're already talking about yours. The question was a launchpad. You asked it to get a turn, not to get an answer.

What got me wasn't the conversational tic. It was that Brooks had cleanly named a thing I've been writing around for two years without a word for it.

Asking a question is the gesture of curiosity. Actually wanting the answer is the substance. Boomerasking is what happens when those two come apart. You keep the gesture and quietly drop the substance. It's hard to catch because the gesture still looks completely correct. Hand someone a checklist that asks "did you ask about their weekend," and you pass. You did ask. The box is ticked. Nothing about the box knows you didn't care.

I don't study conversation. I spend my time on how teams adopt AI, and I've watched this exact failure run for two years in a different costume.

The gesture of AI adoption is usage. Logins, tokens, active seats, a dashboard that goes up and to the right. The substance is whether the way people work actually changed. Those two come apart constantly. Someone opens the new tool, asks it one thing, nods, and goes back to the spreadsheet they've used for nine years. On the dashboard, that's adoption. In reality the old workflow never stopped running. They didn't replace anything. They added a tab.

I've been saying a version of this for a while: you don't have adoption, you just have addition. Boomerasking is the same sentence. You don't have a conversation, you just have two turns.

Once you have the word, you see it everywhere. Training attendance is boomerasking: showing up is the gesture, working differently afterward is the substance, and we keep scoring the room. Compliance is boomerasking: doing what you were told is the gesture, believing it enough to keep doing it when no one is watching is the substance. The gesture is always cheap, visible, and countable. The substance is none of those things. So we count the gesture and go home.

How do you catch the real one?

Brooks has an answer for conversation, and it was the most useful thing she said all hour. The tell for real curiosity is the follow-up question. Not the first question. The second one, the one built out of what the person actually just said. You can't fake a good follow-up cheaply, because it has to be made of their answer. It proves you were there.

My version, for adoption, has the same shape. The tell isn't that something started. It's that something stopped. The spreadsheet got closed. The old report got deleted. The status meeting that existed to do the thing the tool now does got cancelled. Real adoption shows up as an extinction, and you can't fake an extinction. Either the old thing is still open on someone's second monitor or it isn't.

The real thing never shows up as the gesture. It shows up downstream: as a follow-through that had to be built from the last thing, or as an old thing that died. The gesture is a snapshot. The substance is only ever visible as a consequence. We've spent years pointing cameras at the snapshot.

Brooks gave conversation a word for this. The rest of us have been boomerasking our metrics for years and calling it measurement. The fix isn't a better dashboard. It's a better question. Not "did they use it." What did they stop doing. If the answer is nothing, you already know.

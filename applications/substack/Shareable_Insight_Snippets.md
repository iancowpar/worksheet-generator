## 1. Extinction over addition (signature — lead with this)

> Most people measure AI adoption by usage — logins, tokens, active seats. But
> usage looks identical whether someone's genuinely integrated the tool or just
> playing with it. The only signal that actually means adoption is the *old way
> going away*. If someone uses the AI daily but still does the task the manual
> way too, that's not adoption — that's addition. You're paying twice for one
> piece of work. Real adoption is something *stopping*, not something starting.

## 2. The fallback principle (the Slack/Teams story is yours to tell)

> Adoption is slow whenever the fallback still exists. People don't split evenly
> between the old way and the new — under pressure they revert to whatever they
> trust cold. I lived this: an org brought a tool back for one team but left the
> old one running everywhere else, so even people who *wanted* the new one ran
> both. When even the willing get pulled back, the fallback is the structural
> problem — not the people, not the training. So the highest-leverage move in
> any adoption effort usually isn't more training or more champions. It's
> removing the fallback. That's politically harder than running programs —
> which is exactly why most programs never do it.

## 3. The mandate line (safest of all — post as-is, naked)

> The only adoption that lasts is the kind nobody had to order. If it needs a
> mandate to survive, it isn't adoption — it's compliance, and compliance
> decays the second the pressure's off.

## 4. Make the problem unmissable, then get out of the way

> I'd never be the PM who walks into a room with engineering and tells them
> what to build. I show them what's in my head and what the problem is — then I
> get out of the way. The same rule holds for leading people whose craft is
> deeper than mine: you don't out-expert them. You make the problem unmissable,
> point at it, and clear the friction. The job is to sharpen the problem, not
> pre-decide the solution.

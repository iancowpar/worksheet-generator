# Adoption is slow when the fallback exists

*The principle most AI adoption programs are quietly violating — and the Slack/Teams story at every post-merger SaaS company is the proof.*

After a merger I lived through, the company decided to consolidate communication. Slack went away. Teams won.

Then Slack came back.

Veterans of the pre-merger company were thrilled — Slack had been the cultural backbone of how engineering and product talked to each other, and losing it had been quietly painful. Engineering leadership pushed adoption hard. There was a real organizational push to use Slack again, and people genuinely wanted to.

What happened next is the part no one talks about.

Teams stayed in place for everyone outside Product & Technology — sales, services, customer success, finance, the whole non-P&T half of the company. Which meant that even if you were on the P&T side and wanted to live in Slack, you couldn't. You had to run both. Slack for engineering. Teams for everyone else.

I lived this. Slack for my engineering team. Teams for cross-functional work. Two notification streams. Two search histories. Two separate places to lose context.

The interesting thing isn't that adoption was slow. The interesting thing is *who* it was slow for. It was slow for the willing.

---

When even willing users get pulled into parallel workflows, the problem isn't user behavior. It's structural.

Here's the principle I keep coming back to: **adoption is slow when the fallback exists.**

Not "adoption is slow when people are resistant to change." Not "adoption is slow when training is insufficient." Not "adoption is slow when the new tool isn't good enough." All of those can be true. But underneath all of them is something more basic: when there's a working fallback that's sanctioned, available, and frictionless, comfort wins under load. People revert to the familiar when things get hard. And things always get hard.

This isn't a moral failure. It's gravity.

---

Most AI adoption programs are fighting the same gravity, and most of them don't see it.

The manual version of every workflow is still sanctioned. The legacy reports still get circulated. The pre-AI meeting cadence still runs. The old templates still live in the same SharePoint folder. Token usage goes up — leadership sees the line, declares progress — but the old way of working is still right there, fully maintained, with no friction or stigma attached to using it.

The dashboards measure the wrong thing.

Token usage tells you that people *touched* the new tool. It doesn't tell you whether the old way is decaying. And without decay, you don't have adoption — you have parallel workflows, which is the most expensive failure mode of all because you're now paying twice for one piece of work.

---

True adoption isn't *"person uses AI."* It's *"person STOPPED doing the manual version."*

If someone uses Claude Code daily but also still writes specs the old way, you don't have adoption. You have a hobby on top of a job.

The metrics that actually distinguish adoption from theater are extinction metrics:

- Tickets that previously required human triage but no longer do
- Manual reports deleted from the operating cadence
- Meetings cancelled because the AI now produces what the meeting used to produce
- Time spent on the legacy version of a workflow per user, plotted over time

If the legacy version isn't decaying, the new version isn't winning.

---

Which leads to the move most AI enablement programs avoid: **removing the fallback.**

Hard-deprecate the legacy tool with a date. Remove the manual report from the cadence — don't make it "optional," remove it. Stop maintaining the old training materials. Tie performance reviews to using the new workflow, not just to the outcome. Take away the SharePoint folder.

This is politically harder than running programs. Removing things creates immediate, visible resistance. Adding things — training programs, champion networks, recognition systems — looks like adoption work without forcing anyone to give anything up.

So most enablement programs default to programs. Which is why most enablement programs produce parallel workflows: high usage of *both* systems, declining usage of *neither.*

The diagnostic question to ask of any AI adoption push:

*What is the user's fallback if they don't use the new tool? Is that fallback still sanctioned, available, and frictionless?*

Three yeses means your program is fighting gravity. You can run it indefinitely without moving the needle.

---

For the unofficial leaders building real AI adoption — the ones running peer groups, sharing workflows sideways, getting other people to actually use the thing — the work that compounds isn't more enablement. It's helping leadership find the political cover to sunset what people are still falling back to.

That's the harder conversation. It also happens to be the only one that works.

Keep going.

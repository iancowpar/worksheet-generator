# Starbucks AI retirement — warm-distribution strategy

The Starbucks story is a once-a-quarter alignment between current news and
the exact thesis Ian's been writing for a year. The conversation is warm
right now (Alexandra K's post is in active circulation, multiple takes will
hit the same hashtag chain this week). Window is short — 48 to 72 hours
before this news ages out and the next AI failure takes the oxygen.

## The opportunity in plain terms

- **The news.** Starbucks retired its AI inventory counting system nine
  months into an 11,000-store rollout. The internal employee note
  Starbucks themselves published: *"The thought behind it was great, but
  the execution was proving difficult."*
- **Why it's warm.** Reuters first reported worker complaints in February;
  the retirement is this week's news. The story is at the top of the
  enterprise-AI feed. Multiple voices will hit it in the same window.
- **Why it's Ian's lane specifically.** The Starbucks employee note is
  Ian's entire worldview (Zero-Translation Building, extinction over
  addition, "the only adoption that lasts is the kind nobody had to
  order") rendered in fourteen words by a worker at a coffee shop.
- **Side benefit.** A sharper, public proof-of-thinking that any Deltek
  panelist could stumble onto before June 3. Not the reason to publish,
  but a real second-order win.

## The four plays, in order of leverage

### 1. Substack essay (the durable asset)

The primary play. Lives at theunofficialleader, becomes the artifact
everything else points to. Draft below.

- **Length.** 750 words, ~4-minute read.
- **Title.** *The Tuesday Test.*
- **Drop.** Within 48 hours. Friday or Saturday is fine; don't wait
  through the weekend.
- **Why this matters.** A Substack essay is the only piece in this
  sequence that compounds. LinkedIn posts die in three days. The essay
  becomes a permanent reference Ian can cite for the next year and link
  from interviews, the portfolio page, and future writing.

### 2. LinkedIn post linking to the essay

Short. The killer pull-quote plus a one-line setup and a link.

- **Drop.** Within 4 hours of the Substack going live.
- **Hashtags.** Match the conversation — #AIAdoption, #AIStrategy,
  #LessonsLearned. The same chain Alexandra used.
- **Hook.** *"The Starbucks employee note that Starbucks themselves
  published said it in fourteen words: 'The thought behind it was great,
  but the execution was proving difficult.' I wrote about why that
  sentence is the whole pattern."* + link.
- **What this does.** Carries warm LinkedIn traffic to the Substack,
  where it converts to subscribers and durable readership.

### 3. Comment on Alexandra K's post (the warm-direct play)

This is the trickiest move and the highest leverage if executed right.
Done well it's additive to her audience and surfaces Ian to her network.
Done badly it's parasitic. The discipline: add something she didn't
already say, in his own voice, with no link-drop. Let people who want
more find their way to him.

- **Drop.** Same day, ideally before the post ages past 24 hours.
- **Shape.** 2-3 sentences. Acknowledge her framing, add one specific
  thing she didn't, get out.
- **Draft.** *"The line that gets me about the Starbucks story is that
  the workers told them in February. The signal was already in the
  system. What I keep watching is the gap between the floor knowing
  and the org being set up to act on it. That gap is where most AI
  adoption actually fails, and it's almost never the tool's fault."*
- **What it does.** Surfaces Ian to her network as someone who reads
  the same landscape and has a sharper diagnosis. No link, no
  self-promotion. People who want more click his name.

### 4. LinkedIn original post (separate from the comment)

A standalone post in Ian's own voice, in the same window, on the same
news. Different from the essay-link post — this one carries the
diagnosis itself, not just a pointer.

- **Drop.** ~24 hours after the comment, so they don't read as a
  one-two from the same address.
- **Shape.** Medium-length (200-300 words). The Tuesday Test as the
  thesis. Different from the essay; shorter, more pointed, closes on
  the diagnostic question.
- **Risk.** If the essay's already up and the LinkedIn-post-with-link
  has already run, this one risks oversaturation. Judgment call on
  whether to deploy it or skip.

## The disciplines that keep this clean

- **Don't crib Alexandra.** She wrote *"deployment is not adoption,
  speed is not readiness, vendor demo accuracy is not real-world
  accuracy."* Those are her aphorisms. Ian uses the public facts (the
  rollout numbers, the official statement, the employee quote) and
  lands the diagnosis in his own vocabulary.
- **Don't perform indignation.** Quiet diagnosis lands. Outrage doesn't.
  Starbucks actually did the rare thing — they admitted the floor was
  right. That deserves acknowledgment, not a dunk.
- **Don't over-extend.** Ian's worldview is bigger than one news cycle.
  Use Starbucks as the anchor, but don't make it sound like one company
  is the whole story.
- **Don't link-drop in the comment.** The comment is a contribution to
  her thread, not a marketing channel.
- **Don't wait.** 72 hours and the conversation moves on.

## DRAFT — Substack essay

# The Tuesday Test

Starbucks fired its AI inventory system this week. Nine months in,
11,000 stores. The official line called it "a decision to standardize
how inventory is counted," which is a careful way to phrase fixing
something that didn't work. The internal employee note Starbucks
themselves shared was more honest. "The thought behind it was great,
but the execution was proving difficult."

That sentence is the whole pattern.

You can do the thinking well. You can do the deployment well. You can
ship the rollout, run the training, put the dashboard in front of a
leadership team, and still miss the question that actually matters,
which is whether the people who have to live with the system are
different on Tuesday.

In the Starbucks case the workers told them in February. Reuters
reported it. The complaints were specific. The tool was confusing
similar milk types, skipping items, and forcing people to recount
every scan to verify what the AI had said. So instead of saving time,
the AI was making the floor do its job and the AI's job. Starbucks
said the tool was working. Three months later they retired it.

This is the most predictable pattern in enterprise AI right now, and
almost nobody is willing to name it. The deployment goes live and gets
called adoption. The training session happens and gets called
readiness. The vendor demo accuracy gets repeated in slide decks until
people forget it came from a demo. Meanwhile the floor knows. The PM
running their own workflow knows. The engineer who tried the tool
three times and gave up knows. The signal is there. The question is
whether anyone in the chain above them is set up to hear it.

The Starbucks employee note is what hearing it sounds like. "The
thought behind it was great, but the execution was proving difficult."
That isn't a vendor problem. It isn't even a tooling problem. It's an
organization-level problem about which signals you're set up to
receive.

The version of AI adoption that works in practice does the opposite of
what the Starbucks rollout did. It starts closer to the floor than to
the press release. It instruments behavior change instead of activity.
It measures what stopped, not what launched. It pays attention to the
workflows that quietly retired themselves, because those are the only
honest signal that adoption is real.

The underlying frame I keep coming back to is Zero-Translation
Building. The idea is straightforward. If the people who have to use
the system can't operate it inside their own work without translation,
the system hasn't been adopted. It's been deployed. Those are
different things.

The Starbucks tool was deployed across 11,000 stores. It was never
adopted at a single one.

There's an easy way to tell the difference, and most organizations
could run the diagnostic in a week. Pick five workflows your AI tool
is supposed to improve. Ask the people who run those workflows on a
Tuesday whether they're spending less time on them than they did
before. If the answer is no, or worse, more, then what you have is a
deployed system, not an adopted one. Whatever the dashboard says, the
floor is doing both jobs.

The harder question is what to do when the diagnostic comes back ugly.
Most organizations don't have a good answer because the people closest
to the truth are at the bottom of the org chart, and the people
running the program are sitting on a budget line they had to defend to
get funded. Admitting the deployment isn't adoption is admitting the
budget line failed. So the gap widens. The press release stays. The
training keeps running. The dashboard keeps reporting attendance.

This is the part that doesn't get said out loud often enough. The
Starbucks story isn't really about a bad tool. It's about an
organization that found a way to admit, in writing, that the floor was
right. That's the unusual part. Most companies don't reach it. They
keep the tool running because turning it off costs more politically
than letting it underperform.

When the official statement called the retirement "a decision to
standardize how inventory is counted," that wasn't spin. It was, in
its own careful way, the most honest sentence in a corporate AI
announcement this year. They retired a system because the people using
it kept telling them it didn't work. That's the win inside the loss.

The line I'd put on the kickoff-meeting poster isn't the employee
quote, though that one belongs there too. It's a question. Before any
AI deployment, ask the team what would have to be true on a Tuesday,
three months from now, for this to have worked. Then go check.

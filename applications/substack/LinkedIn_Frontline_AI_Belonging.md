## Post

The most interesting AI signal this week wasn't a model release. It was a $17M check written to frontline workers.

Blink — an engagement platform for the deskless 80% — raised $17M from Enlightened Hospitality Investments and announced a partnership with Shake Shack.

Look at who wrote the check. EHI sits in Danny Meyer's orbit — a man whose entire career is one argument: hospitality is the product. When that money goes into frontline software, it isn't a software bet. It's a bet that the frontline worker is the competitive edge, not the cost line.

For two decades, software didn't believe that. We digitized knowledge work first — desks, computers, corporate email, easy ROI. The 80% who don't sit at a desk got the leftovers.

What changed the math isn't the labor shortage. It's AI — just not in the direction people point.

AI is compressing the moat around exactly the work we spent twenty years prioritizing. The deck. The first draft. The routine analysis. Value doesn't vanish when that happens — it moves. It moves to the work you cannot prompt your way to: the shake made right, the patient who felt cared for, the store that felt like someone was home.

That work has a name. It's the frontline.

But "AI can't do it" is only half of why this matters. The other half is what the software is actually for. It isn't communication. It's belonging — giving the 80% a real way to be part of the organization regardless of where, when, or how they work.

It's a thesis I've spent the last two years working on. This week I watched a customer in this space describe their product in almost exactly those words. That's usually the sign a thesis is becoming a category.

So the real question isn't "is white-collar work under attack by AI." It's sharper: AI is repricing work in both directions at once — down for what it can do, up for what it can't. And the most valuable thing it can't do isn't physical. It's relational.

We spent two decades treating the frontline as a cost to manage. AI may be the thing that finally forces us to treat belonging as the edge it always was.

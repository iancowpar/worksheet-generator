## Case Study 1 — The AI Capability Layer

### Opening hook (1.5 min)

Frame the moment before the work started. A PM org with twenty senior
PMs, each carrying a hundred hours of manual prep work a quarter
nobody had time to fix. AI tools had landed in the org with no shape
around them — a license here, a vendor demo there, no shared muscle
for what to actually do with them. Most people were either ignoring
the tools or quietly performing adoption while the real work stayed
the same.

That gap — between AI as installed tooling and AI as actually changing
how PMs spent their week — was the opening.

*[Open question for Ian: what was the specific catalyst? A frustrated
moment? A particular PM's offhand comment? The hook lands harder with
a named human catalyst.]*

### Structure (3.5 min)

The mechanism wasn't a curriculum or a training program. It was
**shared infrastructure + a peer community**, with a personal Claude
Code productivity OS as the seed artifact.

Three components:

1. **The seed artifact.** A personal Claude Code productivity OS of
   twenty custom slash commands Ian had built for his own work. Not
   theoretical examples — actual workflows that had already saved him
   hours a week. The artifact was the proof point. Adoption started
   by other PMs asking *"can I have that one?"*
2. **The founding peer group.** Three to five PMs in a working
   cadence, sharing what they were trying, debriefing what was working
   and what wasn't. Honest about the failures. No slide decks.
3. **The on-ramp ritual.** When a new PM expressed curiosity, the
   move wasn't a training session. It was a 15-minute working session
   where they built their first slash command for a workflow they
   actually hated.

The framework underneath this was **Zero-Translation Building**: if a
PM can't operate the system inside their own work without translation,
the system hasn't been adopted. So everything was built around real PM
workflows, not generic AI capabilities.

### Adoption (3.5 min)

No top-down mandate. No leadership memo. Adoption happened the way
adoption that lasts always happens — through peer recommendation and
lived experience. *"This saved me three hours last week"* was the
distribution channel.

Three patterns showed up:

1. **First-command stickiness.** PMs who built one slash command in
   their first session almost always built a second one within two
   weeks. The on-ramp was the conversion mechanism.
2. **Champion emergence.** Specific PMs — three or four of them —
   went past adoption into authorship. They started building slash
   commands for the rest of the org. They became champions not because
   anyone assigned them, but because they couldn't stop.
3. **Workflow extinction.** The honest signal that adoption was real
   wasn't activity counts. It was *what stopped.* PMs would
   eventually retire whole manual workflows. That was the metric we
   trusted.

*[Open question for Ian: which specific PMs became champions? What
workflows specifically got retired? The Adoption beat needs at least
one named-person story for it to land in the room. Not "a PM" —
"Sarah, who was running her customer feedback synthesis by hand every
Friday, retired that workflow in week three and now runs her own
slash command for it." Need real specifics.]*

### Stakeholders (2.5 min)

Four stakeholder groups, managed differently:

- **PMs (primary users).** Peer-driven. No mandate, no roster, no
  hierarchy. The community was self-organizing because the
  alternative was performance.
- **Engineering (technical partner).** The platform architect
  partnership matters here. The capability layer needed credibility
  with engineering, which came from Ian himself building prototypes in
  Claude Code and scoping with engineering as a peer rather than as a
  PM handing them a brief.
- **Leadership (observers, eventually advocates).** Not pitched. Not
  asked for budget. The work was visible enough that it earned its
  way into leadership awareness on its own. The Brand Champion role
  surfaced it; the Substack thought leadership amplified it.
- **Adjacent functions (UX, Services, Operations).** Curious
  observers. A few specific PMs in those functions joined the working
  group. The community wasn't gatekept.

The discipline: the program never asked anyone for permission, but it
also never operated invisibly. The work was loud about its progress in
the channels that mattered (PM Slack, internal Confluence) and quiet
about itself in the channels that didn't (no all-hands pitches, no
"AI Capability Layer is now official" announcements).

### Measurement / Outcomes (3 min)

The measurement frame was **extinction over addition**: count what
stopped, not what launched.

Concrete outcomes:

- **Twenty PMs in active use** with their own custom slash command
  libraries.
- **Multiple PMs (3-5) became champions for the next wave** of adopters
  without being assigned to.
- **Workflows retired** — specific manual processes that no longer
  happened the old way.
- **The Substack thought leadership emerged from the work** —
  Zero-Translation Building, "the only adoption that lasts is the kind
  nobody had to order," extinction over addition.

What we *didn't* measure (and were honest about not measuring):

- Token usage. Not a meaningful adoption metric.
- Hours saved as a global number. Each PM could quantify their own,
  but the aggregate was unreliable.
- ROI in dollar terms. The program had no budget, so the ROI math was
  also unbudgeted.

*[Open question for Ian: were there specific dollar-equivalent or
hours-equivalent claims you'd want to make here, or do we hold the
measurement story to "extinction" and let the discipline of NOT
inflating the numbers be the credibility move?]*

### Close / bridge (1 min)

The close should bridge to Case 2 by surfacing the lesson: AI
capability is a *behavior change* problem, not a tooling problem. The
next case is the other side of "internal operating mechanism" — not
behavior change, but planning and risk at scale.

## Case Study 2 — The UTA Upgrade-Program Operating System

### Opening hook (1.5 min)

UTA upgrade program. $250M ARR. 300 enterprise customers. A program
lead who needed to know — every Monday morning — which accounts were
actually at risk, what was driving the risk, and where to put their
limited attention this week. The existing tooling answered none of
that. Raw ticket counts gave volume but not signal. Account-level
dashboards existed but lived in silos. Customer health scores existed
but were lagging indicators and gameable.

So the program lead was making weekly prioritization calls on instinct
and escalation noise. With $250M in play. That was the opening.

### Structure (3.5 min)

Three integrated components, designed to work as a single operating
mechanism:

1. **The composite ARR-risk model.** Top accounts scored on weighted
   exposure: ARR magnitude, case volume, true-defect rate, sentiment.
   The weights were tuned through real cases — accounts that escalated
   were retrofitted into the model to see if the score would have
   flagged them. Replaced raw ticket counts with weighted signal.
2. **The five-panel analytics product.** Opinionated by design. Five
   panels covered customer escalations, code defects, internal bugs,
   CVEs, and noise. Embedded a Claude Code function as the routing
   layer — for any given account or issue, it could surface the
   single highest-leverage action across the five panels rather than
   making the user reason across all five themselves.
3. **The weekly reporting cadence.** Automated. Delivered to the
   program lead every Monday morning. Not a dashboard you had to go
   visit — a report that came to you, with the ranked list of what to
   look at this week and why.

Together, the three components turned a noisy multi-source data
environment into a single weekly decision-support artifact.

### Adoption (3.5 min)

Adoption started with one user — the program lead — and that was
deliberate. A program-lead-grade tool needs to be unimpeachable for
the program lead before it gets widened to anyone else.

Three adoption phases:

1. **Single-user trust.** Weekly cadence. Ian sat in the program
   lead's prioritization meeting and watched what they did with the
   report. Adjusted the model and the routing logic based on whether
   the recommendations were actually load-bearing for their decisions.
2. **Engineering as second-tier consumer.** Once the program lead
   trusted it, the routed signal started reaching engineering teams.
   Engineers got specific actions, not dumps of tickets. The signal
   was load-bearing for their week, which meant they paid attention.
3. **Customer-facing teams.** Account managers and CSMs eventually
   pulled the risk scoring into their own escalation conversations.
   The system became reference material for customer conversations,
   not just internal triage.

The deliberate sequencing — single user trust → engineering signal →
customer-facing — was the discipline. A common failure mode for
analytics products is shipping to everyone at once and watching nobody
use it. The opposite worked: ship to one critical user, earn the
trust, expand from credibility.

### Stakeholders (2.5 min)

- **Program lead (primary user).** The only stakeholder that mattered
  for the first six weeks. Everything else was downstream.
- **Engineering teams.** Consumers of routed signal. Managed through
  the platform architect partnership and direct engagement with EMs.
- **Customer-facing teams (AMs, CSMs).** Eventual consumers. Onboarded
  through escalation-room conversations rather than rollout events.
- **Executive escalation rooms.** Indirect stakeholder. The composite
  ARR-risk model started showing up in executive briefings because
  the program lead used it there. Surfaced ~$50M in at-risk ARR before
  escalation — that's the line that earned the program leadership
  awareness.

### Measurement / Outcomes (3 min)

Concrete outcomes:

- **~$50M in at-risk ARR surfaced before escalation.** Before the
  composite risk model, those accounts would have surfaced through
  the noise of raw ticket volume — usually late, after escalation had
  already happened. The model moved the signal upstream.
- **Working defect list cut from ~200 issues to 5 (~97%).** The
  five-panel analytics product with the embedded Claude Code routing
  layer collapsed a sprawling triage surface into a focused
  highest-leverage view.
- **Weekly cadence the program lead actually acted on.** This is the
  load-bearing outcome. Not "we delivered a report" — they used it.
  Every week. Their prioritization conversations referenced it.
- **Replaced raw ticket counts with weighted signal as the
  organizational vocabulary** for talking about account risk.

The measurement framework: the system's value was measured by *whether
decisions changed.* Not whether the dashboard had users, not whether
adoption metrics were green. Whether the program lead made different
calls than they would have without the tool. That was the only metric
that mattered.

*[Open question for Ian: do you want to name the program lead by role
or by name during the talk? Naming a specific person grounds the
story, but adds a Deltek-side question about contacting them.]*

### Close / bridge (1 min)

The close should tie back to the cold open frame (Starbucks): both
case studies are about building internal operating mechanisms that
listen to what the floor and the data are actually saying. Different
flavors — one behavior change, one planning and risk — but the
discipline underneath is the same. Then a one-sentence pivot to
"happy to take questions" and the panel Q&A begins.

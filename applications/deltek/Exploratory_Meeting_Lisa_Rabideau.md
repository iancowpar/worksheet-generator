## What an exploratory meeting actually is

Different from a formal interview:
- Two-way conversation, not Q&A
- Lower stakes on perfect answers, higher stakes on signal/fit
- She's evaluating whether you belong in the active pipeline; you're evaluating whether the role is what you want
- The recruiter pulling you in mid-process is a real signal — you came up as someone worth a look despite there being interviews already underway

Mode: curious, listening forward, willing to share specific opinions when asked. Not pitchy. Don't walk through your resume bullet by bullet — she has it.

## What to bring (have queued, deliver only when natural)

**1. One crisp opinion on AI enablement.** Don't bring three. Bring one and own it. The middle-out adoption thesis from your Substack lands directly on what an AI enablement function is built to do:

> *"Most AI enablement programs default to top-down — policy, governance, mandate. Or bottom-up — let people find their own tools, then govern after. The pattern that actually compounds is middle-out: equipping the people three levels into the org chart who can see exactly where their team's two hours of weekly waste lives, and giving them the tools to fix it themselves. Adoption that spreads peer-to-peer, not down a slide deck."*

This is your single sharpest perspective. If she asks *"what's your view on AI enablement,"* this is the answer.

**2. The AI capability layer story at UKG — framed as a lesson, not a brag.**

> *"I built UKG product's first organized AI capability layer. The thing I learned is that adoption that holds is adoption that doesn't need a mandate. I designed it from the start as shared infrastructure rather than personal tooling, so PMs adopted it because it worked better than what it replaced. We ran peer groups around it to make sure the practice stuck. The lesson I took away: AI enablement isn't a rollout problem, it's a workflow-fit problem — and the only people who can see the workflow clearly are the ones doing the work."*

**3. The Substack — light reference, not pitch.**

If natural: *"I've been writing about this weekly for the last year at The Unofficial Leader on Substack — happy to send the link if it'd be useful."* Don't push. If she asks more, then go deeper.

**4. Curiosity about Deltek's AI moment.**

The whole industry is figuring out enterprise AI adoption. Deltek is in that wave. Show that you understand the moment, and that you're not just looking for any AI job — you're looking at *this* one specifically because of where Deltek is.

## Questions to ask Lisa (pick 4-5)

In rough order of priority:

1. **"What does AI enablement actually mean in your org? What's the scope — internal teams, customer-facing, both?"** Critical because the role title is ambiguous and the answer reveals the actual work.
2. **"Where does this role sit relative to the CPTO and other VPs? Who are the stakeholders the role serves?"** Signals you think org-structurally.
3. **"What does success look like in the first 6-12 months? What would tell you the function is working?"** Outcomes-oriented.
4. **"What's the current AI fluency baseline across Product & Tech? Where would I be starting from?"** Realistic about starting point; signals you understand enablement is meeting people where they are.
5. **"What kinds of profiles have you been seeing in the pipeline? What's been missing?"** Lets her describe the gap — which is where you can offer to fit.
6. **"How long have you been at Deltek? What's been most rewarding about leading this team?"** People-oriented question for a people-oriented leader. Builds rapport.

Pick 4-5. Don't run through all of them like a checklist. Use one to two as openers, let her answer fully, follow her threads, then queue the next.

## What to avoid

- **Pitching hard.** Exploratory means both sides explore. Hard pitching reads as desperate.

- **Talking about competitors.** Don't compare Deltek to other enterprise SaaS by name unless she does.
- **Performative humility or false modesty.** Lisa is a fantastic leader; she'll see through it.

# Panel StoryBrand Stories — June 3

The case study (the AI capability layer at UKG) re-framed through Donald
Miller's StoryBrand framework. Five stories: one for each panelist as
hero, one for the adopters as hero. The guide in every story is the
program — i.e. the work Ian built at UKG, transplanted to Deltek.

## Why this frame for the panel

The default case-study trap is "here's what I did, here's why it
worked." That makes Ian the hero and the panel the audience. StoryBrand
flips it. The panelist becomes the hero of their own story. The program
is the guide that gets them what they want. Ian's job in the room is to
demonstrate he can be that guide — with empathy and authority.

Each story uses the StoryBrand beats:

1. **Hero** (the panelist, specific)
2. **What they want**
3. **Problem** — external (the visible thing), internal (the feeling),
   philosophical (why it matters in the bigger sense), and **villain**
4. **Guide** — with empathy ("I've been in your situation") and
   authority ("here's what I've done about it")
5. **Plan** — three concrete steps
6. **Call to action**
7. **Failure avoided** (the stakes)
8. **Success** (the transformation)

The stories are not slides. They're the architecture under the talk. The
case study should *be built on them* so each panelist hears themselves
without being addressed by name.

## Story 1 — Lisa Rabideau as hero

**Hero.** Lisa Rabideau, VP UX. Twenty years inside Deltek, multiple
functions built from zero, Harmony just launched and rolling into
Costpoint.

**What she wants.** AI adoption inside P&T that works the way UX works
— embedded, persona-tailored, measured by behavior change. Adoption
that's a function, not an event.

**Problem.**
- *External:* AI tools land in the org as toolkits without a function
  around them. Training events don't produce behavior change. Champions
  go quiet after the launch.
- *Internal:* The frustration of watching adoption pitched as a training
  problem when she knows it's a UX problem. The exhaustion of being the
  voice making that argument alone.
- *Philosophical:* AI belongs embedded in the work, the way Harmony is.
  Adoption is part of the product experience, not a separate event.
- *Villain:* Training-as-adoption thinking. "We sent the email, we ran
  the session, adoption is on them."

**Guide.** A program that brings the same persona-research-measurement
discipline to AI adoption that she brought to UX.
- *Empathy:* I've watched the same training events fail. I've been the
  PM who got a tool dropped in their lap and ignored it.
- *Authority:* Built one from zero at UKG. Twenty PMs, no mandate.
  Measured by what stopped, not what launched.

**Plan.**
1. Build the audience map — personas, role-specific use cases,
   maturity tiers.
2. Stand up a champion network with a real cadence, not a roster.
3. Instrument it — measure behavior change, not attendance.

**Call to action.** Run this function the way UX is run. Treat it as
enterprise-wide infrastructure, not a project.

**Failure avoided.** AI adoption at Deltek stalls into PR. Tools
shipped, no behavior change, no narrative for next year's investment.

**Success.** P&T at Deltek genuinely works differently a year from now.
The story Deltek tells at ProjectCon 2026 isn't "we launched AI tools."
It's "here's how our PMs and engineers actually work now."

## Story 2 — Bret Tushaus as hero

**Hero.** Bret Tushaus, VP PM. Trained architect, fifteen years a
firm-IT operator before Deltek, the public voice of Deltek's AI story,
the person personally running AI enablement inside his own team.

**What he wants.** The AI adoption work to scale past his team,
sustained past the novelty phase, without him being the single point of
failure for Deltek's AI culture.

**Problem.**
- *External:* He's run short sprints on real tools with his own team and
  proven the pattern. The next question — how to extend it to the rest
  of P&T without it becoming generic training — is in front of him.
- *Internal:* The fatigue of being the person who has to keep
  evangelizing. The worry that his team's progress doesn't survive his
  personal attention.
- *Philosophical:* AI adoption is culture, not tooling. Cultures need
  infrastructure, not heroes.
- *Villain:* Hero-dependency. Programs that only work because one
  person carries them.

**Guide.** A peer-level function that takes the culture work off his
shoulders and turns it into infrastructure.
- *Empathy:* I've been the single point of failure. The AI capability
  layer at UKG started as me, and the test of whether it worked was
  whether it stopped needing me.
- *Authority:* It did. Twenty PMs running their own slash commands. The
  program became the community.

**Plan.**
1. Inherit the patterns you already built — sprint cadence,
   real-tools-real-problems — and scale them without diluting them.
2. Build a champion network so the next adopter isn't recruited by you.
3. Sunset program elements that have done their job. Measure by what
   gets retired.

**Call to action.** Treat me as a peer who takes the sustainability
problem off your plate.

**Failure avoided.** The Deltek AI story collapses back to "Bret on a
podcast." Your sprints work for your team and never replicate. Dela
messaging runs ahead of internal reality.

**Success.** A year from now, P&T's AI fluency is broad enough that
the customer-facing AI story is backed by an organization that lives
it, not by one VP carrying it.

## Story 3 — Gourav Kapoor as hero

**Hero.** Gourav Kapoor, Sr Director, Cloud Architecture & FinOps. The
person who owns the economics of Deltek's cloud and watches the
AI-tooling line grow inside the bill.

**What he wants.** AI spend that's instrumented, attributed, and tied
to business outcome with the same discipline he brings to cloud cost.

**Problem.**
- *External:* AI tooling costs are growing. Adoption of those tools is
  asserted rather than attributed. There are no unit economics on AI
  investment.
- *Internal:* The discomfort of being the FinOps person watching a
  category get bought without the cost discipline he'd apply to any
  other infrastructure spend.
- *Philosophical:* What you can't measure, you can't optimize. AI
  deserves the same governance as any other line item.
- *Villain:* Hype budgets. Enthusiasm that buys tools without
  attribution.

**Guide.** A program that builds adoption measurement the way FinOps
builds cost attribution.
- *Empathy:* I watched the same dynamic at UKG. Tools bought, savings
  asserted, nobody could prove the math.
- *Authority:* Built a composite ARR-risk model that scored 300
  customers on weighted exposure and produced a number the program lead
  actually acted on. Applied the same discipline to AI adoption —
  "extinction over addition," count what stopped, attribute the saved
  time to the retired workflow.

**Plan.**
1. Define the unit metrics up front — adoption rate per role, hours
   saved per workflow, cost per active user.
2. Attribute outcomes the way you attribute cloud cost — tied to a
   workflow, not asserted on a slide.
3. Report on a cadence the leadership team actually uses.

**Call to action.** Treat AI adoption as a governed program with the
same cost-and-value discipline as any infrastructure line.

**Failure avoided.** The AI line in your cloud bill grows. The
business value stays asserted. In two years someone asks what the
return was and nobody has the answer.

**Success.** AI investment at Deltek can be defended with the same
rigor as any other infrastructure spend. The cost is known, the value
is attributed, the decision to keep investing is informed.

## Story 4 — Michelle Cipollone as hero

**Hero.** Michelle Cipollone, VP PMM. A decade-plus building Deltek's
product story, the Vantagepoint launch on her record, now carrying the
external AI narrative around Dela.

**What she wants.** Deltek's customer-facing AI story backed by an
organization that lives AI in its own work — so the messaging isn't
running ahead of the reality.

**Problem.**
- *External:* Customer-facing AI messaging is exposed if the org behind
  it doesn't live what it says. PMM can write the story; the story has
  to be true.
- *Internal:* The strain of carrying a narrative whose proof depends on
  other people's adoption. Watching adoption happen in pockets without
  a cohesive internal story.
- *Philosophical:* Change is a communication problem before it's a
  tooling problem. The hard part of AI is the human part — she's said
  this on record.
- *Villain:* AI messaging that outruns AI reality. The gap between what
  marketing says and what employees do.

**Guide.** A program that builds internal AI fluency as a story —
segmented by persona, sustained as a narrative, measured by what people
do differently.
- *Empathy:* I've watched marketing get ahead of reality. It's painful
  for everyone.
- *Authority:* Built an AI capability layer at UKG that worked because
  it was a community story, not a tool launch. Twenty PMs, no mandate
  — the narrative carried more than the directive could have.

**Plan.**
1. Segment the audience — PM, engineer, PMM, cloud — and tell a
   tailored story to each.
2. Run the program as a community, not a project, so champions carry
   the story laterally.
3. Build the bridge between internal adoption and customer-facing
   messaging — internal AI fluency becomes external credibility.

**Call to action.** Give me the persona work and the communication
architecture; I'll make sure Dela's customer story is backed by an org
that lives it.

**Failure avoided.** Dela messaging gets dragged down by an org that
doesn't reflect it. Customers smell the gap. PMM has to soften the
story.

**Success.** Deltek's external AI narrative and internal AI fluency
are the same story, told consistently. That's defensible in front of
analysts, customers, and the press.

## Story 5 — The adopter as hero

This is the case study itself, told properly. The PMs at UKG were the
heroes. Ian was the guide. This is the version Cipollone wants and the
version that quietly tells the whole panel what Ian believes about
adoption.

**Hero.** A PM at UKG who hadn't touched AI in their workflow, was
already overloaded with manual prep, and wasn't going to wait for IT
to give them tools.

**What they want.** To get the manual work off their plate without
becoming a "Claude evangelist" or attending another training.

**Problem.**
- *External:* Hours per week lost to mechanical prep — reformatting,
  summarizing, drafting, routing. Calendars full of busywork.
- *Internal:* Quiet frustration that the actual PM work — strategy,
  customer insight, hard prioritization — kept getting crowded out by
  mechanical work that "had to happen first." The feeling of being an
  expensive form-filler.
- *Philosophical:* PMs are paid to think. When the system makes them
  spend the day on the things a machine could do, the system is broken.
- *Villain:* The unchallenged assumption that "this is just what the
  job is." The training-and-launch pattern that says "here's a tool,
  figure it out." The mandate culture that turns adoption into
  compliance.

**Guide.** A peer who'd been in the same workflow, already built the
slash command for the thing they were complaining about, and would sit
with them for fifteen minutes to set it up.
- *Empathy:* I had your week.
- *Authority:* Here's the command, here's the workflow it replaced for
  me, here's the time it gave back.

**Plan.**
1. Pick one workflow you genuinely hate.
2. Set up the slash command together in fifteen minutes.
3. Use it once that week and tell me how it landed.

**Call to action.** Try one. That's it. No commitment past Tuesday.

**Failure avoided.** Another year of manual prep. Another tool sitting
in a tab they never open. Another corporate AI rollout that doesn't
change their Monday.

**Success.** A workflow they actually retired. The version of
themselves who built their *own* slash commands and stopped waiting
for tools to be handed to them. The PM who became a champion for the
next person — not because they were assigned to be, but because they
couldn't shut up about it.

## How to actually use these in the talk

- **The case study spine is Story 5.** That's the only one delivered as
  narrative. The PMs at UKG are the heroes; Ian is the guide. Told this
  way, it does the work of all the other stories implicitly. Each
  panelist hears their own concern answered without being addressed.
- **Stories 1–4 are diagnostic, not script.** They're how you know
  whether a draft of the talk is doing its job. Read each panelist's
  story before each rehearsal — "would Rabideau hear her own want, her
  own problem, her own villain in this version of the case study?" If
  yes, the talk is working for her. If no, what's missing.
- **Use the villains.** StoryBrand villains are the most underused
  element. The villain in each panelist's story is the thing they're
  privately tired of — training-as-adoption, hero-dependency, hype
  budgets, messaging-ahead-of-reality. Name those villains in the talk,
  even glancingly. Each panelist will recognize their own.
- **The transformation is the close.** The case study should end on the
  "success" beat — not on the outcomes ("twenty PMs, ~97% reduction")
  but on the *transformation* the outcomes represent. The version of
  the org that exists after. Outcomes are the proof; transformation is
  the point.

## The four moves

### Move 1 — The function-builder mirror (rapport + credibility, deploy early)

She built the BA discipline, the PM team, the UX Lab, Harmony — all from zero. If there's any opening about her background or the team's history:

> *"I noticed you've built more than one function from nothing here — the BA discipline, the UX Lab, Harmony. Don't credit Harmony to Warren Linscott (press does; it's her UX team's work). Naming Harmony correctly signals you know who actually built it.

### Move 2 — The measurement depth (the half that wins it)

When she asks how you think about measuring adoption (she will — it's literally the role):

> *"Most orgs measure usage. Token counts, active seats, logins. But usage is the same shape whether someone's curious or genuinely integrated — you can't tell adoption from theater with usage data. The metrics that actually distinguish them are extinction metrics: did the person stop doing the manual version? If someone uses the AI daily but still writes the spec the old way, you don't have adoption, you have parallel workflows — the most expensive failure mode, because you're paying twice for one piece of work.*
>
> *And the mechanism underneath that: adoption is slow when the fallback exists. The highest-leverage move an enablement function can make often isn't more training or more champions — it's removing the fallback. Deliberate sunset. That's politically harder than running programs, which is exactly why most programs default to programs."*

Then the lived proof — **the Slack/Teams story** (~60 sec):

> *"The clearest example I have is lived. Post-merger at UKG, the org pushed Slack adoption hard — people genuinely wanted it back. But Teams stayed for non-P&T comms. So even willing users like me ended up running both: Slack for engineering, Teams for everyone else. When even the willing get pulled into parallel workflows, the fallback is the structural problem, not the people. AI adoption is the same shape."*

This is the answer that separates you from every other candidate. It's also exactly her *"the programs that actually change how engineers and PMs work day to day"* — you're answering the question she wrote the role around.

### Move 3 — The persona/role/audience receipts (Lynette's rubric, explicit)

When the conversation touches discovery, segmentation, or "how would you approach the org":

> *"Lynette mentioned you care a lot about knowing the audience, the personas, the role before you design enablement — and that's exactly how I've worked. The five-panel analytics product I built routed by who owns the response, not by severity — escalations to Services, CVEs to Security, code defects to eng leads — because the audience determines the action. The migration tool started with persona work and a UX feedback loop with the Services Consultants who'd actually use it. And when I coached the two PMs on the AI layer, I didn't run a generic curriculum — I configured it for their specific use cases. Role-calibrated, not one-size."*

Naming that Lynette flagged her emphasis is fine and good — it signals the network is real and you listened.

### Move 4 — First-90-days, concrete (she's evaluating whether you've thought about the actual job)

If she asks what you'd do first / how you'd ramp:

> *"First 30 days, before building anything: a fallback-availability audit across the workflows P&T is trying to push AI into.

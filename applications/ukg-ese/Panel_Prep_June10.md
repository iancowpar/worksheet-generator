## What the panel will probe (and how to handle each)

### 1. "How would you partner with BSAs in this role?"

This is the critical question. They're not asking about org chart.
They're asking whether you respect their craft.

> "The way I've worked with analytical partners — at Broadridge with
> the BAs running enterprise onboardings, at UKG with Leila on the
> Jira taxonomy — is co-design, not handoff. BSAs are not translators
> for me; they're the people who actually understand the system
> deeply enough to make the right calls about what's a config issue
> versus a code defect versus noise. My job as PM is to set the
> direction and the constraints, then build the operating model
> together. Where I push is on the front-end discipline — making sure
> what arrives in your queue has gone through real intake, not just
> volume."

Land *"co-design, not handoff."* That's the line.

### 2. "Walk us through how you handle a stakeholder who brings you a solution instead of a problem"

Tasos asked this. Panel likely revisits.

> "Two moves. First, relationships. If I have trust built up with the
> stakeholder, I can ask directly — 'tell me about the problem,
> what's the workflow that's broken' — and I'll usually get a more
> honest read than the original ask suggested. Second, structured
> intake. The request doesn't enter the backlog without a
> justification. Not as a bureaucracy gate — as a way of forcing the
> conversation about the actual outcome. I built that pattern with
> the BEPs at UKG already; it's the muscle I'd want to bring here."

### 3. "Tell us about a time you pushed back on engineering"

The pod-vs-complexity story is gold for this audience.

> "On the UTA upgrade program, engineering wanted to sequence the
> three hundred customer upgrades by database pod — operationally
> efficient for them. My push was complexity-based sequencing —
> easier customers first, harder later, with the schedule resilient
> to the inevitable customer asking us to hold off. Engineering's
> preference was operationally efficient; product's preference was
> operationally resilient. We landed on complexity because the
> delivery date was the load-bearing constraint and only one of the
> two approaches survived contact with customer reality. The
> negotiation worked because I came in with the analysis, not the
> opinion."

### 4. "What's your experience with Salesforce?"

The gap. Tasos already surfaced this — the panel will revisit. Don't
fake it.

> "At an operator level, not a builder level. I've worked inside
> Salesforce for the UTA service request and approval workflows. I
> understand the case management model, the basics of object
> relationships, what configuration looks like versus customization.
> What I haven't done is admin-level work or hands-on Flow building.
> My ramp pattern on platforms I'm new to is the same one I used
> with Leila on the Jira taxonomy — sit with the architect, learn
> the data model, write requirements that respect the platform's
> constraints. The Salesforce depth on this team is what makes the
> ramp credible; my job isn't to out-Salesforce you, it's to bring
> the product muscle to what you already know."

Last line is the move. Doesn't pretend. Acknowledges where the depth
lives.

### 5. "How would you approach AI adoption inside this team?"

Your differentiator. Tasos already loves this. Panel will too.

> "Three principles I'd want to hold to. First, middle-out, not
> top-down. Mandates don't work. What works is finding the early
> adopters, building shared infrastructure they can use, then
> letting peer recommendation do the spread. We did exactly this at
> UKG WFM — twenty PMs picked it up because one PM showed another PM
> a fifteen-minute time-saver. Second, the goal isn't to save twenty
> hours; it's to save ten minutes consistently across thirty
> workflows. Incremental discipline beats grand transformation.
> Third, measure what stopped, not what launched. If I can point at
> a manual workflow that no longer happens, the work was real. If I
> can only point at training attendance, it wasn't."

### 6. "Why come back to UKG?"

The boomerang question. Be honest.

> "Three things. The team and the org context I already know — that's
> a real ramp advantage. The role itself, which is the most
> interesting shape of PM work I've seen — owning demand management
> as the actual product. And family — I'm Lowell-based, the
> community pulls match the work I want to do, and it's the version
> of me I want to be at home."

### 7. "What would your first thirty days look like?"

Listen mode + diagnostic mode.

> "I'd be in listen mode for most of it. One-on-ones with each of
> you, with Tasos, with the BEPs once they're identified, with the
> engineering manager I'd be partnered with. I'd audit the current
> intake mechanism — what's working, what's friction, where the
> escalations come from. I'd watch one or two real intake cycles end
> to end to see the actual flow. Around day fifteen or twenty I'd
> start surfacing observations, not recommendations yet. The first
> month is for getting the picture clear; the recommendations come
> after."

### 8. "What's the biggest risk in hiring you for this seat?"

Honest version, don't deflect.

> "Two risks. The Salesforce ramp time — I'll be running behind the
> team on platform depth for at least three months, and that's a
> real cost. The other is that I'll over-index on the meta-product
> work — designing the intake mechanism, the prioritization
> framework, the measurement — at the expense of the day-to-day
> backlog grooming and story-writing rhythm that the team depends on
> being steady. Both are mitigated by working closely with you for
> the first quarter, but I'd rather name them upfront than have you
> discover them in month two."

That answer is what wins this panel. Honesty about gaps. Specific.
Self-aware.
